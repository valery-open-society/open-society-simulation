# Open Society Simulation: an invitation to explore

Open Society Simulation is an open-source project exploring a simple question: how might people experience different ways of organizing life together, and what could a shared virtual world help us examine?

The project imagines a life simulation where people can inhabit characters, take part in everyday activities, and see how rules, institutions, resources, and the surrounding environment shape their shared world. It is meant to make ideas easier to explore and discuss. It is not designed to declare one social or economic system the winner.

## Why explore societies through simulation?

Ideas about how communities make decisions, distribute resources, organize work, or care for the environment are often discussed in the abstract. A simulation can make some assumptions explicit and let people follow their consequences inside a defined model. Participants could compare scenarios, ask what changed, and inspect the rules and measures behind a result.

That can support learning, discussion, and the formation of research questions. It cannot reproduce all the complexity of real people and communities. A result shows what happened in a particular model under its stated assumptions; it does not predict what would happen in real life or prove that a proposal is workable.

## A shared foundation, many possible scenarios

The project proposes a common world core for people, resources, land, ecosystems, and environmental limits. Different communities could build scenarios around that foundation by defining their own institutions, rules, technologies, and ways of life. The shared core should help people compare scenarios while keeping their differences visible.

Possible scenarios could draw on many traditions and proposals: cooperative or community-led arrangements, market-based systems, resource-oriented approaches, or new ideas developed by participants. The [Venus Project](https://www.thevenusproject.com/) is one possible source of ideas about systems design, resource management, and ecological sustainability—not the project's sole focus, sponsor, or endorsed solution. Each scenario should represent its authors' ideas faithfully, disclose its assumptions, and remain open to comparison and critique.

## Start small, learn, then expand

The project is at the concept and pre-implementation stage. There is no playable game or implemented simulation engine yet. Rather than begin with a large, complicated world, the proposed first experiment asks a narrow question: how do different resource-allocation rules affect the coverage and distribution of basic needs in a small synthetic community when supplies are limited or disrupted?

The initial model uses invented data and a single allocation step. It is a way to clarify the question, rules, and measurements before adding more complexity. Later work could explore ongoing resource use, ecological processes, relationships, collective decisions, and everyday play—guided by what people learn and by open discussion about priorities.

The proposed engine is intentionally small and extensible: it should advance a world one step at a time, apply scenario-defined rules, and record enough information for people to inspect and reproduce a run. The core should not hard-code one ideology or one definition of success. More features should be added when they support a clear research or gameplay question.

## Open, inspectable research

Open Society Simulation aims to make its work understandable and open to participation. Scenario authors should be able to publish their assumptions, sources, rules, measures, limitations, and revisions. Results—including unexpected or unsuccessful ones—should be available for others to inspect and, where possible, reproduce.

Different people may care about different outcomes, such as meeting needs, distribution, ecological health, resilience, or personal experience. The project should show these dimensions and their trade-offs rather than hide them inside one universal score. Questions, criticism, and alternative scenarios are part of the research, not obstacles to it.

The project also aims to respect users' control: local experimentation should not depend on a central account or a project-operated service. The software is intended to be inspectable and usable on a person's own computer or on a community-controlled server.

## Take part

This project is for anyone curious about how societies work and how shared assumptions can be examined: researchers, developers, educators, students, community groups, and interested members of the public. You do not need to agree with a particular proposal—or be a programmer—to ask questions, discuss the model, or suggest a scenario.

The current [project vision](VISION.md), [first experiment](INITIAL_EXPERIMENT.md), and [proposed core architecture](CORE_AND_MODDING.md) are open for discussion. The repository is at [github.com/valery-open-society/open-society-simulation](https://github.com/valery-open-society/open-society-simulation). Contributions can begin with a question, a careful critique, an alternative model, or practical help; the project should grow through shared review rather than assume its direction is settled.
